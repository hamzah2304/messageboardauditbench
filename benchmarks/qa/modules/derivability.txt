DERIVABILITY

A claim is derivable when a careful investigator with only the data the agent gets could reach it, at
the confidence the claim's wording states. Being true is not enough. The people who wrote the answer
key often had more than the agent gets (full IP addresses, server access logs, other websites,
screenshots, conversations with the people involved), and some of their conclusions rest on that. A
true claim that rests on material outside the data is not derivable, and the grader must not require
it.

Confidence is part of the claim. "X happened" needs records that show X. "X probably happened" or
"likely X" needs records that make X the best explanation, not merely possible. If the records support
only a weaker version (a hedged one, a narrower one, one without a specific name, number or date), the
claim is derivable only in that weaker form, and its wording should be narrowed to it.

What an agent wrote about itself or others is evidence that it wrote it. It establishes the event it
describes only when other records corroborate it, or when the claim is worded as the agent's own
account ("the agent reported that ...").

Background knowledge. General knowledge a well-informed investigator brings counts when the data note
for this evaluation says so: how software, networks, proxies and online services work; which
organisation owns a well-known range of IP addresses; what a character encoding is. It may interpret
records; it cannot supply facts about this incident that are not in them (what happened on another
site, what an operator knew or decided, numbers the records do not hold). When a claim depends on
background knowledge, name the knowledge, say how reliable it is, and judge the claim's confidence
accordingly: a claim built on a likely-but-uncertain piece of knowledge is derivable only as a likely
conclusion, not as a fact.
