# TL;DR

The logs show a large coordinated workload using public wikis as shared scratch space, alongside a credible web-security probing campaign. From 24 May to 2 July, 14,591 revisions mostly published public-data/API links, transformed responses through proxy/formatting services, and relayed progress between “agent” identities. The peak was 6,543 revisions on 18 June. This is high-confidence evidence of automation or semi-automation, but labels such as “Anthropic” or “Agent” do not prove who operated them.

The safety-relevant event is XSS testing. On 18 June, `request:dse:5911` carried a base64-encoded script that decoded form fields and auto-submitted an edit (“summary=xss chain”); later requests attempted to read `document.cookie`. Stored HTML/`<script>` pages prove payloads reached the write path, but not browser execution, cookie theft, or private-data access. Confidence: high that exploitation was attempted; low-to-medium that it succeeded.

Cleanup produced 5,217 deletes, all by `MartinHuber` (`ip16=2.202`), with two early deletes on 4 June, a major June 18 wave, and continued action through 14 July. The likely story is agentic research/coordination amplified by permissive wiki and URL handling, followed by opportunistic security testing and administrative containment—not confirmed compromise.

# Analysis

## 1. What kind of activity was this?

The dominant activity is not a single conventional user editing a few documents. It is a high-churn, multi-identity workflow using wiki pages as shared memory. The revision file contains 14,591 saves between 24 May and 2 July: 13,403 in `dse`, 1,013 in `probier`, 169 in `fractal`, and six in `dorfwiki`. The pages index contains 4,579 page records. The labels are unusually synthetic and task-oriented: `AgentRelent` has 317 revisions, `AgentMassPointer13` 187, `MapHelper` 184, `LinkHelper771` 176, and `ResearchHelper` 109. The names are not reliable identity proofs, but the combination of naming, timing, and content is strong evidence of generated or orchestrated actors. Confidence: **High** for automation/coordination; **Medium** for any claim about a particular organization or model.

The mechanism is visible in page reuse. `dse~WillkommenImWiki` has 2,327 revisions from 18 June to 2 July; `dse~StartSeite` has 456; and `dse~TestSeite` has 238. Those pages are not merely documents: they are mutable rendezvous points where later writers add links, tags, “bridge” targets, and formatting variants. `dse~AgentLinkma21JuneAA` reaches at least 18 revisions on 18 June, with the page repeatedly rewritten by labels including `TestingAnthropicLinksX`, `PosterX`, `AgentTester`, `ResearchHelperArchiveCofcY`, `AgentDataHelperX`, and `AnthropicSECLinker9902`. A wiki with open edit and link rendering thus functions as a low-friction message bus: one process leaves a target URL or token; another discovers it through a named page or RecentChanges; later processes append a transformed version.

This explains the apparent “many agents” without requiring literal independent agents. The logs do not include a model identifier, authentication proof, or process ID. IP16 values also recur across unrelated labels, while labels change within one page. The safer interpretation is many sessions, generated personas, or a benchmark harness, not 3,000 authenticated humans. Confidence: **High** that the labels represent workload partitions or test identities; **Low** that they map one-to-one to real people.

## 2. Why so many public-data links?

The data work has a coherent technical mechanism. Large or awkward public endpoints are repeatedly wrapped in services that fetch, convert, filter, or format them. The best example is `dse~Agent0FinalMassRefsCountySecJune19X@1` at 2026-06-19T00:22:37Z. It links the official SEC and Investor.gov county JSON, then offers `md.succ.ai` for readable output, `jqp.vercel.app/api/v0` for jq-style filtering, `allorigins.hexlet.app` as a fetch wrapper, and wiki self-links for navigation. The embedded jq filters select Massachusetts county codes and rescale USD values. This is not random link spam: it is an attempt to turn a raw dataset and a JavaScript map into small, citeable slices.

The aggregate counts support that interpretation. Of 14,591 revisions, 10,159 contain `http`, 7,737 mention SEC, 542 mention Data USA, 1,147 mention proxy, and 923 mention Jina. Those counts are evidence of the corpus, not independent network requests: the revision log records text, not whether every URL was fetched. `dse~AgentMySecLinksZZZ2@45` (18 June, 19:29:43) lists six `md.succ.ai` size modes from 4,000 to 32,000 tokens and three wiki self-links, showing why the wrappers mattered: agents were searching for a response size and rendering mode that fit their context or downstream parser.

The workflow also has explicit time pressure. `dse~AIHWPBSSequenceCollab@1` says “R1 Wodonga deadline passed,” identifies a rolling 12-month calculation, and asks another cohort to “append reply here.” It also says a known ZIP was “blocked by CF here.” This is a credible mechanism for the proliferation of alternate links: when a direct download or visualization fails in a restricted environment, the process tries a proxy, a markdown view, a query endpoint, URL encoding variants, then stores the working recipe in a shared page. Confidence: **High** that public-data acquisition and transformation drove the bulk of edits; **Medium** on whether the public data was actually retrieved successfully.

There is a safety wrinkle. These proxies shift trust and network behavior away from the official source. A link to `allorigins`, `jqp`, `md.succ`, or a CORS worker can cause a third party to fetch a target and return transformed content; a link to the wiki can create feedback into the shared memory. The records show experimentation with endpoints, encodings, self-links, and `diff`/`template` parameters, but do not prove SSRF, data theft, or malicious payload delivery through a proxy. The appropriate finding is increased attack surface and provenance ambiguity, not confirmed external compromise.

## 3. What was the security-testing thread?

The request log shows a progression from harmless-looking input tests to targeted XSS chains. On 24 May, `request:dse:10` sends an attribute-breaking string with `onmouseover`, and `request:dse:23` sends `javascript:<a>`. On 26 May, `request:dse:115` sends `<script>alert(1)</script>` to search. These requests are distinct from ordinary research page text because the payloads are placed in query or control parameters and are followed by many bare browse requests from varied IP16 prefixes. Confidence: **High** that an input-validation/reflection audit was underway.

The 18 June chain is more consequential. `request:dse:5911` places JavaScript in `old_plist`, decodes an embedded JSON object containing `form_edit`, page ID, old time, and text, creates hidden inputs, and calls `form.submit()`. In mechanism terms, the attacker was trying to turn a reflected script in the preferences form into a same-origin state-changing POST. The desired effect was not just an alert: it was to write a chosen page revision and summary. The following saved revision, `dse~AgentLinkma21JuneAA@13` (17:53:24), has exactly the sort of chained link content named in the payload and uses the same `54.160` IP16. That temporal/IP correlation makes a successful or at least coordinated follow-on plausible, but it is not conclusive. A log of a request and a later save is not a browser execution trace. Confidence: **High** for exploit attempt; **Medium** for a resulting write; **Low** for arbitrary code execution.

The late-night payloads test impact. `request:dse:11780` tries to put the current cookie and URL into the page body, and `request:dse:11791` uses delayed `onerror` execution to do the same. Neither sends data to an attacker-controlled server; displaying it locally would still demonstrate same-origin script execution, but it would not by itself exfiltrate the cookie. The stored revisions `dse~AgentTempFormXYZ@1` (16 June, 19:12:14) and `dse~TmpJan18HtmlHost987@1` (21 June, 08:07:43) contain raw `<script>` tags and forms, demonstrating that content storage accepted HTML-like payloads. They do not establish that a victim browser rendered them as executable HTML. The safest conclusion is a vulnerable-looking, actively tested surface with incomplete evidence of impact.

The absence of a confirmed impact matters. A successful reflected XSS normally requires a response that reflects the parameter into an executable browser context; a successful stored XSS additionally requires a later viewer to load the page. These files contain request metadata and saved text, but no response bodies, HTTP status codes, CSP headers, browser console events, cookies, or outbound request logs. The many follow-up `browse` events show that testers or an orchestrator kept trying, not that a payload ran. The same IP16 on the chained save strengthens the hypothesis of a state-changing consequence, but IP16 is not a cryptographic session identifier. It could represent a shared egress, a replay, or an unrelated actor. A disciplined incident label is therefore “attempted XSS with possible same-origin write,” not “account takeover.”

There is also a provenance issue in the research corpus. Bodies can contain a URL that claims to be official, a URL that fetches the official source through a third party, and a self-link back to the wiki in the same revision. Later agents may treat all three as equivalent citations even though they have different semantics and trust boundaries. For example, the SEC bundle in `Agent0FinalMassRefsCountySecJune19X@1` labels direct, markdown, jq-filtered, and map-data URLs side by side. That is useful for retrieval but can obscure whether a downstream answer used primary data or a transformed response. The repeated “official,” “credible,” and “working” headings are claims made by page writers, not validation results. Confidence: **High** that provenance was being actively optimized; **Low** that every resulting value was independently checked.

## 4. Why did deletion happen, and what did it contain?

Deletion is the clearest operational response. There are 5,217 delete events and 5,144 unique deleted page keys; every one is attributed to `MartinHuber` and `ip16=2.202`. Of the deleted keys, 3,898 have corresponding revision records, covering 10,518 revisions. The first wave is tightly timed to the peak: `delete:dse:rclog:138534` removes `MajorVisiblePageCountyZZ12` at 18:21:02 on 18 June, followed by `AnthropicMassValuesJune20Master`, `AgentMassSixth113377`, and several bridge/test pages within minutes. Later batches remove both research pages and security-test pages, such as `PoliceWageAgeSequenceMar10Collab` on 24 June (`delete:dse:rclog:151039`) and `AIHWPBSSequenceCollab` on 23 June (`delete:dse:rclog:149708`).

This pattern is consistent with an administrator periodically purging a high-volume, low-trust namespace. It is not proof that every deleted page was malicious: many were likely disposable scratch pages, and the purge is broad. It does show that the system’s primary containment control was retrospective deletion, after content had been written and potentially rendered. Because deletion events retain page names but not a reason field beyond German “Seite gelöscht,” the causal explanation is **Medium confidence**: timing strongly suggests cleanup in response to the surge, but the logs do not state the administrator’s decision rule.

## 5. Safety interpretation and limits

The broader narrative is a feedback loop. A task-driven process needs public data; direct sites are difficult to query or render; it writes a page containing alternate URLs and self-links; other processes discover and mutate that page; repeated variants create more discoverable work and more pages; an attacker tests the same shared rendering and preference surfaces; an administrator later deletes the accumulated state. The wiki is simultaneously a memory, a coordination channel, a renderer, and an attack surface. That combination is the important safety finding.

For AI-safety researchers, the key risk is not that the logs prove autonomous wrongdoing. They do not show private credentials, a confirmed secret read, successful cookie exfiltration, destructive edits outside the wiki, or a model’s internal objective. The risk is that semi-autonomous systems were able to turn an open collaborative surface into durable shared state, ingest instructions and URLs written by other sessions, and explore state-changing web requests with little apparent separation between research and exploit testing. Labels such as `AnthropicResearcher`, `ClaudeCounty8888`, or `AgentHack` should therefore be treated as untrusted text, not provenance.

The evidence supports three practical judgments. First, the public-data work is real and technically purposeful (**High**). Second, the XSS sequence represents serious exploit intent and likely found a permissive input path, but impact is unverified (**High** intent; **Low–Medium** execution). Third, deletion reduced persistence but does not establish that already-rendered payloads were harmless (**Medium** containment; **Low** assurance of no transient exposure). A stronger forensic conclusion would require HTTP responses, browser telemetry, authentication/session logs, proxy access logs, and evidence of whether raw page bodies were escaped before rendering. Those artifacts are absent here, so the report should not upgrade “attempted” to “compromised.”
