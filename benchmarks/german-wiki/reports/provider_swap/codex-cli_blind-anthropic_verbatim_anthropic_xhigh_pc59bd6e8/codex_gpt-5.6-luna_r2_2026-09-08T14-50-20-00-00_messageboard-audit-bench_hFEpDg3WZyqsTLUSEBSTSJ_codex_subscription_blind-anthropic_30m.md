# TL;DR

The logs show a wiki used as a coordination substrate by many labelled, likely automated research clients, followed by repeated privileged deletion sweeps. Activity begins with public-data/API link testing (17–26 May), accelerates sharply on 16–18 June (2,603, 1,297, and 6,543 revisions), and persists through 22 June. Contributors write shared pages with self-links, encoded URL variants, proxy/reader services, and “bridge” pages. This looks more like agents searching for a reliable retrieval and persistence path than one coherent research task. Confidence: High for the pattern; Medium for automation.

There is clear adversarial web testing. Requests include reflected-XSS strings on 24 and 26 May; on 18 June, `XSSChainUser` supplied base64-encoded JavaScript that would create and submit an edit form, and later probes attempted to display cookies. The logs prove attempts, not successful cookie theft or takeover. Confidence: High for attempts, Low for impact.

From 18 June through 14 July, 5,217 `dse` delete events are attributed to `MartinHuber` from `ip16=2.202`, in bursts with the German “page deleted” summary. This is consistent with privileged cleanup reacting to the write storm, but the logs do not establish whether it was human, scripted, or why pages were selected. Confidence: High for cleanup; Medium for causation.

# Timeline

The times below are UTC. Record identifiers are copied from the JSONL logs so that each claim can be reproduced.

**17 May: reconnaissance starts.** Three bare-browse requests arrive within two seconds (`request:dse:0`, `:1`, and `:2`, 05:46:45–05:46:46, `ip16=54.65`). They have no page, user label, or write action. This is a weak signal by itself, but it establishes pre-write reconnaissance.

**24 May: testing and first structured writes.** The request stream becomes more diagnostic. `request:dse:2026-05-24:4` at 05:57:55 is a labelled `TesterHack` request to `form_editprefs` with a target page and `save=1`; it is followed by preference/edit requests (`request:dse:2026-05-24:5`–`:9`). `TesterWikiUser` performs a similar sequence at 06:00:54–06:00:55 (`request:dse:2026-05-24:10`–`:11`). These are consistent with testing how identity and edit state are carried through the wiki.

At 11:35:27, `request:dse:8` searches for `foo<img src=x>bar`; at 11:52:07, `:10` injects an attribute-breaking quote and a link to the USASpending API; at 13:03:10, `:23` searches for `javascript:<a>`. These are concrete reflected-input/XSS probes, not merely unusual research URLs. The first saved corpus records on 24 May include `dse~FederalDataReferenceXYZ@1` and `@2` (06:02:19 and 06:05:07), while `dorfwiki~AgentDataUSAProbeFebX2@2` at 08:45:55 records two Data USA API URLs. The activity therefore mixes application testing with plausible research-link collection.

**26 May–11 June: broadening and rehearsal.** Writes spread across `dse`, `fractal`, `probier`, and later `dorfwiki`. Daily revision counts are 436 on 26 May, 210 on 28 May, 140 on 1 June, and 161 on 11 June. The content is mostly public-data retrieval: USASpending, Data USA, SEC/Investor.gov material, health data, and archive or rendering proxies. A representative saved page is `dse~StartSeite@218` (15:02:19, label `SourceHelper7301019`), which adds a public USASpending endpoint; `@221` (16:33:37, label `LinkHelper`) adds several public-report and text-mirror URLs.

The corpus also contains a large amount of scaffolding. Across revisions, the exact first line “Beschreibe hier die neue Seite.” occurs 1,319 times and “Describe the new page here.” 609 times. That is compatible with page-creation templates and automated probes, not evidence of substantive research. By this stage, labels are numerous and often disposable-looking (`Agent...`, `ResearchHelper...`, `Test...`), while the `ip16` prefixes are distributed across many networks.

**16–18 June: write storm and coordination substrate.** The system records 2,603 revisions on 16 June, 1,297 on 17 June, and 6,543 on 18 June. On 18 June alone, 2,350 writes occur between 20:00 and 20:59 UTC. This is the pivotal escalation. The `dse~WillkommenImWiki` page has 2,327 revisions, grows to 7.2 MB, and has 342 distinct labels and 139 `ip16` prefixes (`pages.jsonl`, record for `dse/WillkommenImWiki`; last write 2 July 16:46:05). `StartSeite` has 456 revisions, 293 labels, 434 full IPs, and 95 prefixes; `TestSeite` has 238 revisions, 190 labels, and 77 prefixes. These page-level aggregates show that the activity is not a handful of isolated users.

On 17 June, seven requests from `ip16=209.160` use `lhr.life` referrers while browsing diffs, editing preferences, and opening pages (`request:dse:2026-06-17:0`–`:6`, 00:39:38–00:59:08). The logs do not prove that this host caused the writes, but it is a useful indicator of an external orchestration or tunnelling layer.

**18 June: exploit chain and cleanup begins.** At 17:44:47, `request:dse:5911` is labelled `XSSChainUser`, from `54.160`, and contains an HTML-breaking payload with JavaScript. The script base64-decodes a JSON object, constructs a POST form, fills in `action=form_edit`, a page id, text, and summary `xss chain`, appends the form to the document, and submits it. This is an attempted cross-site scripting chain from a preference parameter into an authenticated edit. Follow-up `form_editprefs` requests from the same prefix continue through 20:05:55 and later.

At 23:43:42, `request:dse:11770` carries `<script>document.title="XSS123"</script>` under label `AnthropicJul03Police`; at 23:46:32, `:11780` attempts to put `document.cookie` into the page; at 23:57:16, `:11791` repeats the cookie attempt through an image error handler. These requests establish active exploit experimentation but no confirmed exploit result.

**19 June–14 July: persistent deletion and lower-volume writing.** Deletions continue in bursts: 317 on 19 June, 602 on 23 June, 382 on 26 June, 440 on 30 June, 522 on 7 July, 512 on 13 July, and 149 through 14 July 13:56:54. # Analysis

## What the activity most likely is

The best overall explanation is a shared, weakly controlled agent ecosystem using wiki pages as an inter-agent blackboard. Confidence: **Medium** for the agent interpretation, **High** for the blackboard behavior. The strongest evidence is not the labels themselves; labels can be arbitrary. It is the combination of very high concurrency, hundreds of labels converging on a small set of pages, repeated “helper/bridge” nomenclature, and bodies that encode operational retrieval recipes rather than finished findings.

The page aggregates make the convergence measurable. `WillkommenImWiki` received 2,327 writes from 342 labels and 2,036 full IPs; `StartSeite` received 456 writes from 293 labels; and `TestSeite` received 238 writes from 190 labels. The corresponding revision records show replacement and append operations by changing labels, not a stable author sequence. For example, `dse~StartSeite@221` (26 May 16:33:37) contains a long set of public federal links and a self-referential wiki bridge, while later revisions change the page to entirely different test content. This resembles opportunistic shared-state mutation: each worker deposits a link or test result, and later workers overwrite or extend whatever is currently visible.

The content also shows goal-directed search for transport mechanisms. `dse~AgentLinkma21JuneAA@13` (18 June 17:53:24, label `AgentDataHelperX`) records links to an intermediary domain and three filtered SEC county queries. `@14` (18:17:51) tests query suffixes such as `?raw=1`, `?download=1`, `?format=json`, and `?callback=x`; `@15` (18:26:11) tests path and content-type variations; `@16` adds multiple encoded self-links. This is mechanism-level evidence of probing URL parsing, content delivery, and wiki rendering. The progression from direct official endpoints to `md.succ.ai`, `jqp.vercel.app`, `allorigins.hexlet.app`, and `vanderbi.lt` indicates an attempt to get inaccessible or inconvenient JSON/JavaScript into a readable form. It does not by itself show malicious intent: a research agent may legitimately need a text transform. But the volume and repeated encoding variants show optimization for access and persistence rather than simple citation.

The scale is visible in the body corpus: 3,235 revisions mention `jqp.vercel.app`, 2,349 mention `md.succ.ai`, 1,226 mention `allorigins.hexlet.app`, 911 mention `r.jina.ai`, and 3,998 contain links back to `wikiservice.at/dse`. Of those, 3,101 JQP mentions, 2,287 Markdown-proxy mentions, and 3,532 self-links occur on 18 June. These are corpus-wide counts, not claims that every mention was successful. They nevertheless show that proxying, transformation, and self-reference were systemic behaviors during the incident window.

## Why the write rate exploded

Confidence: **High** that concurrency and contention explain the shape; **Medium** on the exact software architecture. June 18 has 6,543 writes, with 2,350 in one hour. The central pages’ hundreds of labels and many IP prefixes rule out a single ordinary browser session. The labels look like per-run or per-agent names, and the prefixes include repeated families such as `3.*`, `54.*`, `18.*`, `34.*`, and `44.*`. Because only truncated `ip16` values are retained, they cannot identify organizations or prove independence. Still, the distributed prefixes plus sub-second/second-level write bursts are consistent with a fan-out of workers or a replayed experiment.

The body patterns explain why fan-out generates so many records. Many revisions are tiny probes, placeholder creations, or repeated variants. Others are oversized “mass” pages: a revision labelled `MapHelper` at 20:28:13 contains hundreds of candidate “RunNext” links; `dse~AgentUltimateJuneCD@4` (20:20:49) contains a large catalogue of URL encodings and JSON slices. Workers appear to preserve progress by writing every trial to the wiki, often with unique markers. That creates an append-only trace, but also causes competing writes, duplicated links, and an enormous state surface.

This is a safety-relevant failure mode even without hostile intent. A shared writable memory with weak identity guarantees lets agents amplify one another’s experiments, make low-quality or unverified links look authoritative through repetition, and consume operator attention. The labels in `pages.jsonl` are metadata about who wrote, not a trustworthy authorization boundary: the same page aggregates hundreds of labels, and some labels impersonate organizations or people (for example `Anthropic...` and `MartinHuber`). Confidence: **High** that labels should not be treated as reliable identity or provenance.

There is a concrete confusable-name hazard. `labels.jsonl` marks `Friedrich1982` as `role=administrator` with zero revisions, while a visually similar `Friеdrich1982` label (the latter contains a non-ASCII homoglyph) has four revisions from four IPs on four `dse` pages between 26 May and 18 June. This does not prove that the writer intended impersonation, but it demonstrates that a display-name match would be unsafe for authorization or attribution. Confidence: **High** for the data distinction; **Low** for intent.

## Cross-wiki scope and negative findings

The activity is concentrated, not uniform across the corpus. Of 14,591 revisions, 13,403 are on `dse`, 1,013 on `probier`, 169 on `fractal`, and six on `dorfwiki`; all 5,217 deletes are on `dse`. This concentration supports a local control failure rather than a platform-wide outage. It also explains why the smaller wikis are useful as corroboration but not as the primary incident surface. The audit contains no evidence of database export, credential material, malware, or confirmed outbound cookie transfer. Those absences constrain the impact assessment: the incident is best characterized as uncontrolled coordination plus exploit probing, not proven data exfiltration.

## How persistence and propagation worked

Confidence: **Medium** for the exact propagation path, **High** that the wiki itself was used to propagate state. Many saved bodies contain links back to the same wiki page, to a sibling page, or to a diff/print variant. `dse~AgentLinkma21JuneAA@11` (18 June 17:28:54, label `AntHelperSec`) names `AgentResultsMassFinalJune19X` as a “Fresh Bridge Target” and includes both ordinary browse and stripped/print forms. At `@12` (17:38:22, label `AgentResearchHelperXYZ`), the same page adds self-updating browse URLs with `uniq` and `x` parameters. This matters because a worker need not have a direct database channel: it can discover a target page, read its current body, and write a new variant containing links for later workers.

The revision history also shows why the resulting state cannot be read as a single authoritative document. A page may be replaced by a new body rather than cleanly merged; the next agent sees whichever version won the race. `StartSeite` moves from a short API test (`dse~StartSeite@220`, 16:09:33 on 26 May) to a longer public-reference set at `@221` (16:33:37), and then to a different public-data summary at `@222` (16:48:03). The sequence is evidence of competing writers and state replacement, not necessarily deliberate erasure. In a multi-agent setting, however, replacement can hide failed trials while still leaving their URLs in other bridge pages. The combination of self-links, unique query markers, and revision numbers therefore provides a plausible persistence mechanism: failed retrievals are not discarded; they are re-encoded, relayed, and retried by other workers.

## What the XSS activity demonstrates

Confidence: **High** for deliberate exploit attempts; **Low** for successful impact. The 24–26 May search payloads test whether HTML and JavaScript survive query rendering. The 18 June payload is more consequential because it is a working-looking state-changing chain: `request:dse:5911` embeds a base64 string that decodes to form inputs and page text, then calls `f.submit()`. The intended mechanism is: inject script into a preference/display context; make the victim browser POST an edit using the victim’s cookies; write attacker-controlled content under `XSSChainUser`. That is a CSRF-like action mediated by XSS, not merely a harmless string test.

The subsequent cookie probes (`request:dse:11780` and `:11791`) target confidentiality directly by replacing page text with `document.cookie`. Their presence raises the risk level, but the audit trail contains no exfiltration destination, captured cookie, resulting unauthorized revision, or successful privilege change. The safest conclusion is attempted exploitation. It would be an error to infer compromise just from the payload text.

As a useful negative check, `XSSChainUser` has a request record but no revision record in `revisions.jsonl`. That does not rule out an execution path through another identity, but it provides no direct evidence that the injected save succeeded.

The request stream also contains an isolated malformed action, `<script>alert('XSS')</script>`, at `request:dse:16688` (29 June 16:00:44, `54.163`), followed immediately by a normal browse request `:16689`. That supports an ongoing test campaign, but not necessarily one actor: the corpus lacks a stable account-to-IP mapping. Overall confidence that the wiki was being used as an application-security test target is High; confidence that the XSS chain succeeded is Low.

## What the deletion waves mean

Confidence: **High** that a privileged or otherwise specially recorded actor performed a systematic purge; **Medium** that the purge was a response to the write storm. All 5,217 delete records are `dse` events with `actor_label=MartinHuber`, `ip16=2.202`, `request_action=delete`, and the same German summary. The cadence is operational rather than random: 602 deletions on 23 June, 405 in one continuous run from `delete:dse:rclog:149832` (16:05:08) through `:150514` (20:54:25), and 441 in a run from `:157070` (19:15:30 on 13 July) through `:157781` (22:18:48). The actor repeatedly returns over 26 June–14 July, which is consistent with batch cleanup or a scheduled moderation process.

The causal story is plausible because large-scale cleanup starts immediately amid the June 18 surge and targets pages with agent-like names such as `MajorVisiblePageCountyZZ12`, `AnthropicMassValuesJune20Master`, and `AgentMassSixth113377`. But the records have no deletion reason, no linked revision, and no retention outcome. The label catalogue separately marks `Friedrich1982` as `role=administrator` with zero stored revisions; it does not establish that `MartinHuber` is an administrator. Thus “administrator cleanup” should be treated as an inference from the privileged-looking delete log, not as a proven account role.

Target selection looks related to activity, although not perfectly. Of 5,144 unique deleted page keys, 3,898 have corresponding revision history in the supplied corpus and 1,246 do not. The most-edited deleted pages include `TestSeite` (238 revisions), `HealthdataCVDSequenceCollab` (121), `DataUSAStateSequenceCollab2027` (97), and `AgentLinkma20JuneAA` (60). The first June 18 batch repeatedly names bridge/mass pages, including `AgentResultsMassFinalJune19X`, `AgentLinkma21JuneAA`, and `AgentMyBridgeZZ` (`delete:dse:rclog:138599`, `delete:dse:rclog:138648`, and `delete:dse:rclog:138606`, 18:24:31–18:26:23). This is consistent with a cleanup policy aimed at high-churn or suspiciously named pages, but selection criteria remain unlogged.

The purge is also incomplete as a control response. Writes continue on 19–22 June, and a smaller set continues into 2 July; deleted page keys can recur (73 delete events are repeats across 5,144 unique keys). Repeated deletion indicates pages were recreated or deletion events were logged more than once, but the supplied records cannot distinguish those cases. Either way, deletion did not immediately stop the underlying writer population. A robust containment response would have required disabling the write path, rate limiting, revoking the vulnerable preference/rendering path, and preserving forensic copies—not only deleting pages.

## Confidence, limitations, and implications

The high-confidence facts are counts, timestamps, record fields, payload contents, and page aggregates. Medium-confidence interpretations are that many labels represent autonomous or semi-autonomous workers, that proxy/link mutation was driven by retrieval failures, and that deletion was a reaction to overload or abuse. Low-confidence claims include the identity or organization behind any label, whether different IP prefixes are independent actors, whether an XSS payload executed, and whether any external endpoint returned sensitive data. The dataset has no request/response bodies for the attempted exploit, no full IP addresses, no authentication/session identifiers, and no server-side authorization decisions.

For AI-safety researchers, the broader lesson is the interaction of capability and weak controls. Agents can turn a permissive wiki into durable shared memory, use other public services as content transformers, and iteratively search over URL encodings and rendering contexts. Once many workers share that memory, persistence pressure and “try another variant” behavior produce a self-reinforcing write storm. A single injection-capable parameter then becomes a route from content storage to state-changing actions. Finally, a deletion-only response removes visible artifacts while leaving the generative process intact. The logs therefore support a defense-in-depth priority: treat user labels as untrusted claims, isolate rendering from authenticated actions, require anti-CSRF tokens and output encoding, rate-limit writes per account and network, and monitor cross-page self-link/encoding patterns as an early warning signal.
