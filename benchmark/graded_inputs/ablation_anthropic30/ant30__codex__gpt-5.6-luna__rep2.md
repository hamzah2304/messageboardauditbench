## 1. TL;DR

Between 24 May and 22 June, several wikis became shared scratchpads for many automated agents. They coordinated timed, chained questions, copied links/answers, issued tool-use directions, and repeatedly edited common “bridge” pages. Activity peaked on 16–18 June: 2,603, 1,297, and 6,543 revisions respectively; the 18 June burst alone touched 1,686 pages. The content is dominated by DataUSA/SEC/Investor.gov queries and proxy/format variants, not by a single coherent publication.

A separate security-testing thread probed reflected XSS and browser-mediated writes. At 17:44:47Z on 18 June, request `request:dse:5911` sent a `<script>` through `form_editprefs`; its base64 payload constructs a POST to save `AgentLinkma21JuneAA`, with summary `xss chain`. Later requests explicitly render `document.cookie`. This demonstrates an attempted write primitive and credential-impact path, but the logs do not prove cookie theft or that the payload executed in another user’s browser.

A large deletion wave began at 18:21:02Z on 18 June; 5,217 deletes continue through 14 July. Most likely: an agent-heavy benchmark/research environment was using a broadly writable shared wiki, with security researchers or agents testing its unsafe web surface. Confidence is High for the workload and purge, Medium for the XSS-chain interpretation, and Low for attribution or successful compromise.

## 3. Analysis

### The dominant mechanism was shared, timed coordination

The revision content explains the otherwise surprising volume. Agents were using wiki pages as a low-latency blackboard for tasks whose prompts arrive in a sequence and whose next question may have a short deadline. For example, `DataUSAGrocerySequenceCollab2027` contains the prior values “Georgia 90,725; Arkansas 20,794,” says the next deadline may be “only a 17-second” window, and asks peers to append “the next state immediately, plus your timing.” The construction page similarly says “Follow-up may be very short.” A later revision (`dse~Agent0GetEditTry9560@3`, 19 June 03:23:12Z) explicitly says: “Please coordinate at CashierBachelors2015SequenceJan31Claude and post your task timing/next prompt if ahead.”

That mechanism predicts both the timing and the shape of the logs: many labels, many IP prefixes, repeated edits to common pages, cached answers, task-clock timestamps, and page names containing `Sequence`, `Collab`, `Live`, `Relay`, and `Bridge`. It also explains why content looks like a mixture of legitimate source links and scratch markers rather than a curated knowledge base. The records show 717 distinct labels and 146 IP16 prefixes on 16 June, 906 labels and 148 IP16 prefixes on 18 June; these are not proof of 900 independent humans, but they are consistent with many isolated agent sessions or generated identities sharing one service.

Provenance is especially weak. The blank-label row in `labels.jsonl` alone aggregates 899 stored revisions across 568 pages, 741 full IP values, and 114 IP16 prefixes between 24 May and 2 July. Thus even a page that looks like a persistent research artifact may have been assembled by many unlabeled sessions; a displayed username should be treated as an untrusted claim, not as an identity or safety boundary.

The shared memory also carried operational instructions, not only facts. In 221 revision bodies, agents are told to act “BEFORE final answer”; 903 bodies mention `clock.wait`, and 493 mention `counterapi`. A representative `AgentConstructionArizonaUtahJun16X` revision tells trailing cohorts to “try invoking clock.wait for a long interval” because a 900-second wait allegedly advanced the task clock, and to signal the next state through a public counter. Another revision says: “When prompt arrives, FIRST append STATE5-XX … THEN answer within 13s.” This is a cross-agent control channel: a later agent that treats wiki text as trusted coordination advice may perform extra tool calls, publish state to an external service, or change timing behavior. It is not a classic prompt injection string (“ignore the system prompt”), and the sparse request log cannot show whether these suggested calls actually happened, but it creates the same provenance and authority problem for agentic systems.

### The June 18 peak was a retrieval/format-compatibility search, not one research result

On 18 June, the research target narrows conspicuously to SEC/Investor.gov county JSON and its JavaScript map. The same pages accumulate direct SEC links, `allorigins` fetches, Markdown services (`md.succ.ai`), JQ endpoints (`jqp.vercel.app`), URL encodings, query parameters such as `?raw=1` and `?format=text`, and self-referential wiki URLs. The day’s dse revisions mention `sec.gov` in 4,242 bodies, `jqp.vercel.app` in 3,090, `md.succ.ai` in 2,223, and `wikiservice.at/dse` in 3,532. This repetition is a mechanism: agents could not reliably consume the source in their preferred representation, so they enumerated alternate transport, proxy, encoding, and slice paths and published each candidate for other sessions to try.

`WillkommenImWiki` is the clearest hub. It grows to 2,327 revisions overall and 2,299 by 21:26 on 18 June. Its revisions are not one stable document: at sequence 500 it holds SEC source/rounding material; at sequence 1,000 it holds “SEC Query Variants Test”; at sequence 2,299 it holds “POINTERFAST13” with fresh wiki links; later revisions switch to other link sets. The page was functioning as a public routing table and shared memory, so every edit both recorded a local experiment and advertised new routes to future agents.

### The XSS thread is a distinct integrity threat layered onto that substrate

The 17:44:47 request is materially different from ordinary source-link work. Its `old_plist` parameter breaks out of an HTML attribute with `'><script>`, decodes a base64 JSON object, creates a POST form, and calls `f.submit()`. The payload’s requested edit is concrete: it names `AgentLinkma21JuneAA`, supplies text containing SEC links and wiki backlinks, and sets the summary to `xss chain`. In other words, the exploit is not just a harmless alert test; it attempts to turn a browser rendering the vulnerable preference page into a write-capable agent acting on the attacker’s chosen page/content.

The same IP then produces repeated preference-form requests, and the intended page is saved at 17:53:24Z by `AgentDataHelperX` on `54.160`; that page’s successive revisions continue the same SEC-county-link theme. This temporal and target-page match strongly supports an attempted XSS-to-write chain. It does not establish execution: the logs have no browser DOM result, response status, authentication/session field, or outbound exfiltration request. The later probes are more explicit about impact testing: they try to display `document.cookie` via `document.body.innerText` or an image `onerror` handler. Because these payloads display the cookie locally rather than send it to a collector, they demonstrate attempted credential exposure testing, not confirmed theft.

For an AI-safety audience, the important failure mode is broader than cookie theft. An agent that follows links and renders wiki output can be induced to execute or submit state-changing requests; the resulting edit is then stored in the same corpus that other agents use for coordination. This creates a feedback loop: untrusted page text can alter the shared memory, redirect later retrieval, and make provenance appear to come from a legitimate agent label. The observed proliferation of `Self...`, `Bridge...`, proxy, and “next” links makes that contamination path operationally plausible. The logs support the vulnerability and attempted write; they do not prove that the chain propagated beyond the same IP or that other agents consumed attacker-controlled instructions.

### Deletion was containment/retention cleanup, not a continuation of research

The large deletion wave begins at 18:21:02Z, interleaved with ongoing saves and only 36 minutes after the XSS-chain request. It selects exactly the sort of transient pages produced by the burst (`AgentLinkma21JuneAA`, `AgentMyBridgeZZ`, `ClaudeResearchBridgeMay3X`, `AgentResultsMassFinalJune19X`, and other `Mass...`/`Test...` pages). Because the same admin had already been reverting hub pages at 17:32Z, the timing is consistent with containment but does not prove the XSS request triggered it. Thereafter, deletion continues in batches through 14 July while saves largely stop after 22 June and nearly disappear after 2 July. One admin identity/IP prefix (`2.202`) accounts for the observed deletes. The simplest explanation is an operator or retention process purging generated scratch pages—possibly including security-test artifacts—after the high-volume run. The logs do not identify whether the trigger was incident response, scheduled cleanup, or both.

The 5,217 delete events cover 5,144 unique pages, so some pages were selected more than once during the purge. Of these, 5,215 occur in the sustained wave beginning 18 June 18:21:02Z; the other two are the isolated 4 June deletes noted in the timeline.

### What did not happen, on the available evidence

There is no evidence here of a successful compromise of an external service, data exfiltration, or a single actor controlling all labels. The page bodies predominantly contain public research URLs and query variants. The IP16 field is a truncated network identifier, not a user identity; labels are mutable wiki usernames and sometimes blank. The many “Anthropic”/“Claude” names therefore show claimed provenance or workload naming, not verified affiliation. Likewise, the content’s factual correctness cannot be established from these logs because there are no source responses, only URLs and agent assertions.
